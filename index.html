Breaking Federal Policy / Legal

Trump Administration Appeals Harvard Funding Ruling, Seeks Reinstatement of $2.7B Freeze at First Circuit

Harvard Federal Policy Legal

The Trump administration has filed a sweeping 160-page brief with the First Circuit Court of Appeals, asking the court to overturn the September ruling that struck down its freeze on Harvard's research funding. The Harvard Crimson reports that the administration called on the First Circuit to reinstate its roughly $2.7 billion freeze on Harvard's research funding, arguing that it acted within its authority when it terminated the University's grants. The brief rests on three core arguments: that the district court lacked jurisdiction, that Title VI does not exclusively govern funding terminations, and that the April 2025 demand letter did not violate the First Amendment. Federal lawyers argued that agencies retain authority under the terms of grant agreements to terminate funding when it no longer "effectuate[s] program goals." Separately, Higher Ed Dive reported that the DOJ filed a standalone lawsuit in March accusing Harvard of being deliberately indifferent to campus antisemitism, seeking both repayment of awarded grants and a permanent cut to future funding, including Pell Grants and student loans.

Sources: Harvard Crimson (Apr. 16, 2026) • Higher Ed Dive (Mar. 20, 2026) • Harvard Magazine (May-June 2026)
Implications for Brown Brown has already reached its own settlement with the administration. The Harvard case remains the defining legal test for whether federal agencies can use grant termination as ideological leverage outside of Title VI procedures. A First Circuit ruling against Harvard would dramatically expand federal coercive power over all research universities, including Brown's $785M+ annual sponsored research portfolio.
Competitive Intelligence Harvard's continued litigation stance means its researchers face ongoing uncertainty and potential talent migration. Brown, having settled, is in a relatively stable position to recruit faculty from institutions still in legal or financial limbo. Monitor for Harvard faculty departures to peer institutions.

Breaking Campus Safety / Institutional

Brown Announces Barus and Holley Renovation Plan, Security Expansions Following Dec. 13 Shooting

Brown Campus Safety Institutional

President Christina Paxson announced this week a "full and transformational renovation" of the classrooms closed since the December 13 mass shooting that killed two students and injured nine others. The Brown Daily Herald reports that the University is in the process of renovating 10 Barus and Holley classrooms that were closed after the Dec. 13 shooting, with the goal of having the rooms available for use in the middle of the 2026-27 academic year. These rooms represent roughly 10% of the University's 105 registrar-controlled classrooms, and 33% of the large classrooms on campus. Security camera coverage will be expanded over the summer to buildings housing sensitive research and critical infrastructure. Global consulting firm Teneo is simultaneously conducting an after-action review and a campus-wide safety and security assessment, with key results to be shared with the campus community in August. Separately, the Brown Daily Herald reported that criminal extortion group ShinyHunters claimed responsibility for a cyberattack on Brown's Canvas learning management system.

Sources: Brown Daily Herald (May 7-8, 2026) • Brown University News (May 8, 2026) • WPRI/WJAR (May 8-10, 2026)
Implications for Brown Admission Prospective students and families will have questions about campus safety during visit season and Commencement Weekend (May 22-24). Admission staff should be prepared to speak to concrete safety investments, the Teneo review timeline, and the renovation plan. The Class of 2030 admitted students tour campus at a moment of active healing and visible institutional response. Messaging should be empathetic, concrete, and forward-looking.

High International Enrollment / Immigration

State Department Reports 8,000 Student Visas Revoked Since Trump's Return; Broader Campaign Against International Students Continues

Harvard Columbia Brown Cornell Penn MIT Stanford All Ivies International

According to Inside Higher Ed, the Department of State has revoked 8,000 student visas since the start of President Donald Trump's second term. In total, the administration has revoked 100,000 nonimmigrant visas, which is about double the number revoked in former President Joe Biden's last year. A survey by the Presidents' Alliance found that interest in U.S. postgraduate education has plunged 40 percent since January 2025 while there is rising interest in Germany, France, and China as a study destination. Enforcement actions have included mass SEVIS record terminations, expanded social media vetting for all F, M, and J visa applicants, and a "one-strike" proposal from Secretary Rubio. The Trump administration has also encouraged universities to voluntarily cap international student enrollment and is working toward regulatory changes that would make it more difficult for international students to study in the U.S.

Sources: Inside Higher Ed (Jan. 13, 2026) • NAFSA (Apr. 10, 2025) • Presidents' Alliance (Updated Mar. 2026)
Implications for Brown Admission Brown's international student community (~13% of undergrad enrollment) faces direct legal vulnerability. The 40% drop in prospective international student interest is a structural enrollment threat. Brown's admission office should monitor pipeline data from key feeder countries (China, India, South Korea, Canada, UK) closely and be prepared to answer international family questions about SEVIS stability, social media vetting, and travel advisories in counseling interactions.
Competitive Intelligence Peer institutions across the board face the same headwinds, but schools that have settled with the administration (Brown, Columbia, Cornell) have somewhat more SEVP stability than Harvard, which remains under active DHS scrutiny. This is a potential differentiation point in international recruitment.

High Institutional Finance / Federal Policy

New Tiered Endowment Tax Takes Effect in 2026, Forcing Doctoral Cuts and Hiring Freezes at Harvard, Yale, Stanford, MIT

Harvard Yale Princeton MIT Stanford Duke Penn Institutional Finance

The "One Big Beautiful Bill Act" passed by Congress in July 2025 created a new three-tier endowment excise tax that takes effect this fiscal year. Per The Christian Science Monitor, schools with endowments of $750,000 to $2 million per student will pay a 4% tax, and those with endowments of more than $2 million per student will pay 8%. The American Enterprise Institute estimates that Harvard could owe as much as $368 million in taxes, while Yale could owe $276 million. Princeton could face a $217 million tax bill, and Stanford $202 million. In a notable development, the Daily Princetonian reported this week that Princeton's investment company disclosed the university may be exempt from the tax because its number of tuition-paying students falls under the 3,000-student threshold -- a result of its recent expansion of financial aid. Yale's Graduate School dean confirmed the school is cutting doctoral admissions targets across the board in response. Stanford has already laid off 363 staff members and cut its operating budget by $140 million.

Sources: Christian Science Monitor (Feb. 26, 2026) • AEI (Jul. 14, 2025) • Daily Princetonian (May 10, 2026) • PBS NewsHour (Aug. 8, 2025)
Implications for Brown Brown's endowment per student is well below the $500,000 threshold, so Brown is not subject to the new endowment tax. This is a meaningful financial advantage relative to wealthier peers. However, peers cutting doctoral programs and financial aid due to the tax could affect Brown's graduate recruitment landscape and the pipeline of teaching faculty.
Competitive Intelligence Yale is cutting doctoral spots; Stanford has laid off staff; Harvard and MIT face nine-figure annual tax bills. Brown's relative financial stability -- combined with its Trump settlement -- may make it a more attractive destination for faculty and graduate students priced out of or destabilized by peer institutions. Brown's admission office should be aware that peer financial aid programs (especially Yale's new $0 tuition threshold for families under $100K) raise the competitive bar for affordability.

High Legal / Federal Policy

University Settlement Scorecard: Columbia Paid $221M, Cornell $60M, Brown $50M in Workforce Commitments; Harvard Alone Still Fighting

Columbia Brown Cornell Penn Harvard Dartmouth Federal Policy

As of spring 2026, the landscape of university settlements with the Trump administration has largely crystallized. Inside Higher Ed's analysis notes that as of April 1, 50 out of 64 cases remain ongoing, and only 30 have reached appeals. Columbia agreed to pay a $200 million settlement to the federal government over three years, and $21 million to settle investigations involving the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission. The Cornell Daily Sun confirmed Cornell has agreed to give $30 million to the federal government and invest an additional $30 million into research to strengthen U.S. agriculture over the next three years, restoring over $250 million in frozen funding. Per CNN, Brown University reached a deal in which it would pay Rhode Island workforce development organizations $50 million in grants over 10 years. Penn settled by revising its transgender athlete policies without a cash payment. Fox News reported that President Donald Trump suspended federal funding to every Ivy League school except the University of Pennsylvania and Dartmouth College amid the antisemitism investigations. Harvard remains the sole institution actively litigating against the administration.

Sources: Inside Higher Ed (Apr. 3, 2026) • Cornell Daily Sun (Nov. 7, 2025) • Columbia Office of the President (Jul. 2025) • PBS/AP (Jul. 2025)
Implications for Brown Brown's settlement -- $50M to RI workforce development over 10 years -- was among the least financially burdensome of all Ivy settlements, and crucially preserved academic independence and admissions autonomy. This positions Brown favorably: federal investigations are closed, funding is flowing, and the university avoided the reputational cost of either capitulating (Columbia) or prolonged confrontation (Harvard). Brown Admission should be conversant in these distinctions when speaking to prospective students and families.
→ Read: Inside Higher Ed
Watch Institutional Finance / Admissions

Yale Expands Financial Aid to Cover Full Tuition for Families Earning Under $200K, Effective Fall 2026

Yale Admissions Financial Aid

In a January announcement that takes effect for the incoming Class of 2030, Yale announced significant enhancements to its financial aid program. The changes eliminate all expected costs for families with typical assets and annual incomes below $100,000 while also ensuring that families with typical assets and annual incomes below $200,000 receive need-based scholarships that meet or exceed the cost of tuition. Yale reports that by raising the threshold to $100,000, nearly half of all American households with children ages 6-17 would now qualify for a financial aid package that does not require parents to contribute anything. Under the new policy, more than 80% of American households would be eligible for a Yale scholarship covering at least the cost of tuition. This announcement comes even as Yale simultaneously faces an estimated $280 million annual endowment tax burden and has implemented hiring slowdowns and doctoral admissions reductions.

Source: Yale University News (Jan. 27, 2026)
Implications for Brown Admission Yale's new $0-tuition threshold for families under $100K and tuition coverage up to $200K income is a direct competitive challenge for Brown in middle-income recruitment. Brown's current financial aid program covers 100% of demonstrated need but uses a different income-threshold framework. Brown Admission should audit its aid communication and net price messaging against Yale's new standard immediately. Families with incomes between $75K-$200K are now Yale's explicit target demographic.
Competitive Intelligence This is the most significant peer financial aid expansion in the current cycle. Brown should assess whether its own financial aid benchmarks and messaging need to be updated in response. Notably, Yale is making this investment despite an 8% endowment tax looming -- a signal of competitive urgency around middle-income access.

Watch Enrollment / Demography

Enrollment Cliff Arrives: 18-Year-Old Population Begins Projected 15% Decline; Application Volume Still Growing at Selective Schools

All Schools Enrollment Admissions Trends

The widely anticipated demographic contraction has arrived. According to CollegeData, 2026 marks the beginning of a drop in the number of 18-year-olds in the U.S., a demographic shift dubbed the enrollment cliff, and the steepest declines are projected to drop by 15% by 2029. However, Common App data from Pioneer Academics shows that Common App reported 1.43 million first-year applicants through March 1, 2026, a 2% increase year over year. The divergence between a shrinking traditional-age population and rising application volumes at elite schools reflects what one analysis calls "application inflation," with a record high of 6.59 applications per student putting strain on institutional resources. Selective institutions continue to see growing applicant pools due to global demand and Common App platform effects, while regional and smaller schools in the Midwest and Northeast are most exposed to the cliff.

Sources: CollegeData (Jan. 2026) • Pioneer Academics (Apr. 2026) • Cengage/National Student Clearinghouse (Feb. 2026)
Implications for Brown Admission Brown, as a highly selective institution, is partially insulated from the enrollment cliff -- domestic and international demand for Brown continues to outpace supply of seats. However, the cliff will reshape the geographic distribution and demographics of the pool over the next five years. Brown should accelerate recruitment in growing Sun Belt markets and among first-generation and lower-income students as the Northeastern high school graduate cohort shrinks.
→ Read: CollegeData
Watch Institutional Finance / Research

NIH and NSF Funding Slow to Flow Even After Congress Rejected Proposed 40-55% Cuts to Science Budgets

Harvard Brown MIT Stanford Princeton Cornell Duke Vanderbilt Research Funding

In February, Nature reported that weeks after the US Congress rejected unprecedented cuts to science budgets that the administration of US President Donald Trump had sought for 2026, funding to several agencies that award research grants is still not freely flowing. The White House had proposed slashing the NSF budget from $8.8 billion to $3.9 billion (a 56% cut) and cutting NIH by nearly 40%. While Congress rejected these proposals, the administration has used other levers -- including indirect cost caps at 15%, grant terminations, and agency reorganizations -- to constrain university research. The administration's FY2026 budget also proposed setting the maximum Pell Grant award at $5,710 for the 2026-27 award year, a $1,685 decrease from the current maximum award, and eliminating the Federal TRIO programs and Supplemental Educational Opportunity Grants entirely.

Sources: Nature (Feb. 27, 2026) • Inside Higher Ed (May 5, 2025) • AAU (Jun. 2025)
Implications for Brown Even with its settlement intact, Brown's research enterprise is subject to the same NIH/NSF indirect cost cap and grant uncertainty as every other research university. Brown's School of Public Health and Warren Alpert Medical School are particularly exposed to NIH variability. The proposed Pell Grant cut, if enacted in a future budget cycle, would directly affect Brown's low-income enrollment strategy. Monitor FY2027 budget negotiations closely.
→ Read: Nature
